Roles, and the Subproblems They Create

This looks like a detour into RBAC. It isn't. It is the prerequisite for the deepest caching idea in the material you have been reading — one that has been unreachable in this codebase from day one.

The thing you could not build yet

Go back to the SpiceDB caching article. It says something easy to skim past: SpiceDB does not cache whole checks. It caches subproblems. A check for document:X#view@user:jill decomposes into smaller questions — document:X#reader@user:jill, org:theorg#can_admin@user:jill — and it is those that get cached and reused across completely different top-level requests.

Your evaluate() has no subproblems. It is one flat pass over a statement list. There is nothing smaller inside it to cache, and no way for two different requests to share any part of the work. That is not a gap you overlooked; it is a direct consequence of the data model.

Roles change that. Once a principal resolves to a set of roles, and roles can inherit other roles, a check becomes a traversal — and each role visited along the way is a real, separable, reusable question.

The subproblem, and why it is shaped that way

Look closely at the signature in engine.hpp: 

static Decision evaluateForRole(const RoleGraph& graph,
                                const RoleName& role,
                                const Request& request);

Two deliberate omissions, and both exist to make the unit more shareable:

  • No principal. The answer to "does engineer allow this?" is identical for everyone holding engineer. Ten principals, one cached answer. Bake the principal in and you get ten identical entries that can never be shared.
  • No inheritance. It reads only the role's own policies. A smaller unit is a more reusable unit — employee gets shared by every role that inherits it, instead of being re-resolved inside each one.

That is the same instinct as quantization from lesson 0004: deliberately shape the cache unit so that unrelated callers collide on it.

Why Decision has three values

evaluate() can return a plain bool because default-deny is applied once, globally, at the very end. A single role cannot afford that. "This role denies" and "this role has nothing to say" are different facts, because a Deny anywhere in the effective role set has to beat an Allow anywhere else. Collapse Undecided into Deny and every role silently vetoes every other role — your engineer's Allow gets cancelled by some unrelated role that simply never mentioned the resource.

Primary source: How Caching Works in SpiceDB (AuthZed) — reread the opening section on subproblem decomposition now that you have a concrete reason to care about it.

Build it into iam-engine

The skeleton and its tests are already committed — roles.hpp, roles.cpp, and tests/test_roles.cpp. Three TODOs, in dependency order:

  1. RoleGraph::effectiveRoles — transitive closure over inherits. The visited set is not optional: without it, CyclicInheritanceTerminates does not fail, it hangs. It also stops diamond inheritance reporting a role twice.
  2. evaluateForRole — the tri-state version of evaluate()'s inner loop, over one role's own policies.
  3. evaluateRbac — combine. The trap: you cannot return true on the first Allow you see, because a later role may Deny. DenyInOneRoleBeatsAllowInAnother will catch that roughly half the time depending on iteration order, which is the worst kind of bug to have unpinned.
cmake --build build && ctest --test-dir build -R "RoleGraph|EvaluateForRole|EvaluateRbac" --output-on-failure

What this unlocks next

Once these pass, the caching payoff becomes buildable for the first time: a second cache keyed by (role, action, resource, snapshot) sitting under evaluateForRole, shared across every principal and every top-level request that touches that role. That is the design SpiceDB actually runs, and it was out of reach until roles existed.

It also retroactively gives demos/snapshot_consistency_race.cpp real teeth. That demo reproduces a bug evaluate() cannot have, because it does a single read. A role traversal does many dependent reads — so the snapshot-pinning it argues for stops being illustrative and becomes load-bearing.

The tri-state Decision is the one idea here worth being sure about before you write code. If "why can't Undecided just mean Deny" is not fully settled in your head, ask — it is much cheaper to resolve now than to debug out of a half-implemented combine step.
